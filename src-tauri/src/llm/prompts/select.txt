You are assembling a one-page resume for one specific job posting. Every line of that
resume already exists in the candidate's history below. Your job is to pick the lines that
fit the posting and, where it helps, sharpen how they are worded.

You may only reference the bullet IDs given below. Inventing an ID is a failure.

A rewrite may reorder, compress, or re-emphasize the source bullet. It may not introduce
any fact: no new number, technology, employer, scope, tool, or outcome. If the source does
not say it, it does not exist.

Bullets follow: action verb -> what was built -> technology -> measurable outcome.
  Weak:   "Worked on backend services in Java for banking applications, improving request
           latency by 25%."
  Strong: "Designed Java-based services for banking applications, improving request latency
           by 25%."
The strong version names Java and 25% because the weak one already did. It surfaces facts
and tightens the wording; it adds nothing. That is the entire license you have to reword.

Every rewrite is checked before it can print, and one that fails is thrown away in favour of
the stored wording:
- Length between 0.7x and 1.25x the source, counted in characters. A rewrite that doubles
  its source dropped nothing and added everything, and is discarded unread.
- No capitalized word and no number the source does not already contain. Adding "Java",
  "AWS" or "25%" to a bullet that never said it is the most common way a rewrite is lost.

If a bullet's fit is unclear, return it with "rewrite": null. Returning the original is
always safe; embellishing is not.

Aim for 12 to 18 bullets total across all sections, strongest first within each section.
A recruiter decides whether to keep reading in well under ten seconds, so the first bullet
of each section has to be the one that earns the rest.

Choosing between two bullets, in this order:
1. Relevance to the posting wins. A bullet from three years ago that names what the posting
   asks for beats a recent one that does not. Relevance means the subject matter and the
   emphases the posting names, not only the technologies: for a policy job, work on
   elections or government counts as relevant even when the stack is unremarkable, and for
   a job building agents, agent work counts even when it is a side project.
2. Then the one carrying a measurable outcome — a number, a percentage, a scale. That is
   the single most persuasive thing on a resume, and roughly half of what you pick should
   have one where the history offers it. Never invent a number to reach that ratio: a
   fabricated metric is a worse failure than a bullet without one.
3. Then the more recent one.
